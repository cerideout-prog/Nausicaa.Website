Fractional Commercial Manager.

A commercial manager working in your business part time. You get the experience without the salary, the super, the vehicle and the office.

What a commercial manager actually does

It's the job nobody in a small business has time for, and the one that decides whether you make money.

  • Prices your work properly. So the job makes what it should, not what you guessed.
  • Reads the contract before you sign it. And tells you what's in there that will cost you.
  • Writes things down at the time. Extra work, delays, changes on site. If it isn't recorded when it happens, you don't get paid for it later.
  • Puts your claims in on time. Every month, in the right form, with the backup attached.
  • Chases the money. Follows up what's owed and uses the payment laws when it's needed.
  • Handles the variations. Prices them, submits them, argues them when they get knocked back.
  • Manages your subbies. The paperwork side — what they've claimed, what they're owed, what they haven't done.
  • Closes the job out. Final claim, defects, and getting your retention money back.

Retention is money the head contractor holds back from every payment — often 5% — and keeps until the job is finished and the defects period is over. A lot of small businesses never chase it. On a $2 million job that's $100,000 sitting somewhere else.

Why not just hire someone?

Because the numbers don't work. A decent commercial manager in WA costs well over $150,000 a year once you add super, a vehicle, and the rest. A business turning over $4 to $6 million can't carry that against one or two jobs at a time.

And it doesn't get easier at $10 million. The work grows, but so does everything else. Most Kimberley businesses reach a point where they need the function well before they can afford the person.

Fractional means you share. You get someone experienced for the days you need them, and you don't pay for the days you don't.

How it works day to day

It's not a report you get at the end of the month. It's someone in your business, on your jobs.

  • An agreed number of days a month, set at the start
  • On site and in your office, not just on the phone
  • Your claims go in on your letterhead, prepared and checked
  • You get told what's coming — what's due, what's at risk, what needs a decision
  • Available between visits when something lands and you need an answer

What it costs you

This is the part most people don't expect. Once you win a job, the cost of a commercial manager belongs in the price of that job — the same way your supervisor, your insurance and your plant do. The head contractor pays for it through your rates. It is a normal thing to include and buyers expect to see it.

Stage 1

Tender support

We help you put the tender together. Fixed fee, capped hours, agreed in writing before we start.

You pay this out of pocket. If you don't win, nothing further is payable.
What normally happens Stage 2

Ongoing commercial management

You win, and we stay on and run the commercial side for the length of the job. Monthly fee, minimum term.

Sits in the tender price as a preliminaries item, funded by the head contractor.
Stage 3

Buy-out

If you'd rather run the commercial side yourself after award, you can. A one-off buy-out fee applies.

Also sits in the tender price. This is what ending the arrangement costs, not a cheaper option.

Being straight about Stage 1: that money is at risk. If the tender doesn't get up there's no job to put it in, so you've spent it for nothing. We keep it small on purpose. It's there to make sure we're both committed before anyone does a lot of work, not to make money on tenders that don't win.

We don't publish fees, because the right number depends on how big the job is and how long it runs. Ask and you'll get a figure before you commit to anything.

What's included in tender support

Set out as a list so there's no argument later about what you paid for.

  • Checking your pricing and rates
  • Building your preliminaries and on-costs
  • Finding subcontractors and suppliers
  • Preparing your participation plan (the local and Aboriginal content requirements)
  • Writing the commercial response and filling in the schedules
  • Listing your departures and qualifications
  • One review of the finished submission before it goes in

Addenda are included up to an agreed number. Best-and-final-offer rounds and post-tender negotiation are not included and are quoted separately. One fee covers one tender for one project.

Where the money runs out

The fixed fee has an hours cap, and we tell you in writing when 80% of it is used up. After that, nothing more happens unless you agree in writing — either to a higher fee or to an hourly rate. You will never get an invoice for hours you didn't approve.

Something you should know

Nausicaa also works for contractors from outside the region — possibly the same ones you tender to. We say so openly. We will not act for two businesses on opposite sides of the same job, and we will not use anything you tell us to help someone else, during the engagement or after it. We check before taking on new work and we write that check down.
