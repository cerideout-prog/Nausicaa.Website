Why local content is a compliance risk, not just a policy goal

WA Government contracts above certain thresholds now carry mandatory WA Industry Participation Strategy (WAIPS) commitments. Larger contracts trigger Aboriginal Procurement Policy (APP) obligations alongside the Social Procurement Framework. These are not aspirational targets — they are contractual obligations with reporting requirements, and failure to meet them at close-out has commercial and reputational consequences.

The structural problem is that most contractors writing WAIPS and APP participation plans have never worked in the Kimberley. They nominate suppliers who do not have the capacity to perform, set targets against a supplier base they have not verified, and then spend the project managing the gap between what they promised and what is actually available.

A participation plan written with current Kimberley market knowledge sets achievable targets, nominates suppliers who can actually deliver, and builds the reporting mechanism before work starts — not after the first progress claim.

The Kimberley supplier landscape

The Kimberley does not have the supplier depth of the Pilbara. The contractors who succeed at local content obligations understand the difference between what is on a supplier register and what a business can realistically perform. That distinction matters when setting targets that will survive close-out scrutiny.

What exists in the Kimberley supplier market: labour hire, civil plant operations, logistics and transport, camp catering and facilities, security and site services, and a growing number of Aboriginal-owned businesses with specific capability in civil, earthworks and land management works. What does not exist: complex structural steel fabrication, specialist electrical and instrumentation, and most trade categories requiring regulatory licensing at scale.

Participation plans built on this understanding set targets in the right categories, engage the right suppliers early, and produce close-out reports that hold up.

What a credible participation plan contains

A WAIPS participation plan is not a table of supplier names. It requires a description of the works, identification of WA industry capability in each trade package, a methodology for engaging WA suppliers, measurable commitments with reporting milestones, and a mechanism for addressing gaps if targets are not met. Plans that do not address each of these elements are returned for revision — and revision takes time that a project programme does not have.

Aboriginal participation — a different conversation

APP compliance requires more than identifying an Aboriginal-owned business and directing work to it. The obligation extends to structuring the engagement so the business can perform — which means understanding its capacity, ensuring the subcontract terms are appropriate, and supporting any capability gaps that would otherwise prevent delivery.

This requires a conversation that cannot be had from a Perth office. Nausicaa Consulting maintains current working knowledge of Aboriginal businesses operating in the Kimberley — their capability, their capacity constraints, and the kinds of subcontract structures that allow them to participate without taking on risk they cannot manage.

How we manage conflicts

Local content planning is buy-side work — advising the contractor or principal on how to meet obligations. Business capability coaching for Aboriginal and regional businesses is sell-side work. Nausicaa Consulting does not perform both services on the same project. The conflict check is written, conducted before engagement, and disclosed to affected parties.
