Project Profile — Mining & Civil Services / Organisational Scaling

Building the Corporate Structure to Hold Rapid Growth

A general management tenure that took a Kimberley field-maintenance business into contract mining, civil construction and transport — and built the functions, systems and commercial discipline to make that growth hold.

Executive Summary

The client is a Kimberley business working in mining, civil and maintenance contracting. When Callum joined it was a field-based maintenance labour business run directly by its founders. Within months it had won a waste mining package at a major Kimberley mining operation and scaled rapidly — growth arriving faster than the business had structure to absorb. Callum was brought in as General Manager specifically to build that structure: reporting lines and KPIs, commercial ownership, policy and HSEQ systems for disciplines the business had never delivered, and standalone HR, recruitment, commercial and marketing functions where none existed. He left with the business operating from a purpose-built Kimberley facility, running civil and transport divisions alongside its maintenance core, and holding multi-year contracts priced and negotiated under his commercial authority.

The Challenge

When Callum joined, the business was built on one thing done well: supplying skilled tradespeople to remote mine sites. Its corporate infrastructure was minimal and entirely appropriate to that size — an office manager handling rostering, a director running payroll, and the Managing Director making most operating decisions directly. There was no HR function, no finance function separate from the family, no commercial function, and no defined reporting structure.

Then the growth arrived. A New Year's Eve bid for that waste mining package was won, and the business scaled rapidly within months, hiring operators in volume and mobilising equipment from multiple suppliers. At the same time the business took on a tailings storage facility construction contract another contractor had left behind, entered civil construction, stood up a Main Roads-registered transport company, and moved into production mining — disciplines it had not previously delivered.

The risk was structural rather than commercial. A business can win work faster than it can build the systems to price it, staff it, govern it and get paid for it. Rapid headcount growth without a corporate spine produces the failures that end contractors: unpriced risk in tenders, inconsistent pay and rostering, uncontrolled hiring, no HSEQ system for work outside the original scope, and no reliable view of project profitability.

Callum's Role

Callum was General Manager, reporting to the company's two Managing Directors.

The role carried an unusually heavy commercial weight for a general management position. Callum held tender authority and hiring decisions, and owned profit forecasting, commercial modelling, and the commercial and account relationships across the client base.

His direct reports were the Operations Manager, the Recruitment and Talent Lead, the commercial function, site supervisors and external consultants. The Workshop and Asset Manager reported to him with a dotted line to the Directors. He led and managed the HR function without being its direct line manager — a deliberate separation, keeping employee relations independent of the executive making hiring and commercial decisions. Finance reported directly to the Directors as an internal family function.

Approach

Company structure, reporting lines and KPIs

Callum designed and implemented the organisational structure the business had not previously needed: defined divisions, reporting lines, and KPIs against which functions and projects could be measured. He then led a RACI process across teams and projects to settle who was accountable for what — the necessary follow-on once a structure exists on paper.

Building functions from nothing

Every corporate function the business now runs was built during this period. HR, recruitment and talent, commercial and marketing did not exist as functions when Callum joined; the work was absorbed by an office manager, a director and the Managing Director. Callum built each as a discipline with its own ownership. HR and finance, previously handled internally within the family, now operate as standalone functions with dedicated hires.

Commercial ownership and contract renewal

With tender authority, Callum owned pricing and commercial structure across the contract base. Three engagements show the scope: appointment as a Kimberley local government's tier-one plant and machinery maintenance provider on a multi-year term; a multi-year extension of the road maintenance contract at that same mine site, where he led the contract review, pricing, commercial model and contract format; and the extension of a second remote-site engagement from a fixed workshop into the supply of operators and equipment alongside the existing mobile workshops.

Policy, procedure and HSEQ for new disciplines

Moving into mining, civil and transport meant operating under obligations the business's maintenance-focused systems did not cover. Callum led and implemented the operational policy suite, safe work procedures, competency assessment documents and project HSEQ functions the new work required.

Local workforce and training pathways

The Kimberley labour market is small and heavily contested, and most major clients in the region carry local employment requirements. Rather than treat local content as a compliance line, Callum built the recruitment and talent function around it, and worked alongside one of the Managing Directors with North West Regional TAFE on training pathways based in the region. The business is Indigenous co-founded, and its workshop team is Indigenous-led under the Operations Manager, who reported to Callum.

Pay bands, systems and data discipline

He developed a pay band calculator built on skills, experience and port of home, replacing case-by-case negotiated pay with a defensible, data-driven framework. In a labour market where the business competes with major resource projects for the same people, consistency in pay is both a retention tool and a cost control. On the finance side his role was to advise rather than own: the migration from MYOB to Xero and the establishment of general ledger cost tracking against projects were driven within the finance function, with Callum consulted on both.

Outcomes

  • ✓Workforce scaled, then right-sized. Scaled up for a fixed-term mining contract and back down to a sustainable permanent base without a disorderly demobilisation.
  • ✓New capabilities established. A civil construction division, a Main Roads-registered transport company, and a period of production mining delivered on the mine-site contract.
  • ✓Multi-year contracts secured under his commercial authority. Local-government tier-one plant and machinery maintenance (multi-year); mine-site road maintenance extension (multi-year); a second remote-site engagement extended into operators and equipment.
  • ✓Corporate functions now standing on their own. HR, recruitment, commercial and marketing established as functions; HR and finance moved out of internal family administration into standalone teams with dedicated hires.
  • ✓Equipment availability sustained through the scale-up. Availability is the product the business sells — it is what keeps client projects running — so holding it through a period of rapid headcount growth and new disciplines is a direct measure of whether the operating systems held.
  • ✓Local and Indigenous employment held through the scale-up. The workforce on the mine-site contract stayed substantially Kimberley-local and Indigenous through the period of fastest hiring — the point at which local content is hardest to hold — and the apprentice intake grew over the tenure.
  • ✓A permanent home. The business relocated to a purpose-built workshop and headquarters on its own site in the Kimberley.

Why It Matters

Growth is the easy thing to point at; whether a business survives it is a different question. This profile shows an executive hired specifically to build the structure that makes fast growth durable — organisational design, commercial control, governance systems and functional capability — in a business that had none of it and needed all of it at once.

The commercial weight of the role is the distinguishing feature. Tender authority, pricing, profit forecasting and account ownership sat with the same person accountable for building the operating structure, which is why the two stayed connected rather than drifting apart. And the more telling outcomes are not the peak headcount: they are that the workforce came back down to a sustainable base without disorder, and that equipment availability held throughout.
