Market intelligence

The WA & Kimberley project pipeline, 2026–2027.

Major public and private investment is building a substantial pipeline of infrastructure, energy and community projects across WA and the Kimberley. The opportunity isn't just to wait for the tender — it's to see where the work is coming from early enough to position for it.

Last reviewed August 2026. Figures below are as announced or budgeted at time of writing — treat them as indicative and verify current status against the linked source.

In short: track government infrastructure programs and major project announcements, then map the contractor, subcontractor, logistics and support-service opportunities they create. The strongest opportunity for a Kimberley business is often not the headline project itself — it's the enabling works, subcontracting, logistics, accommodation, equipment, labour and maintenance that sit around it.

Western Australian infrastructure investment

$38bn state infrastructure pipeline

The WA Government's 2025 State Infrastructure Programme identifies a $38 billion pipeline over four years across hospitals, schools, community facilities, transport and utilities — creating demand well beyond the head contracts, in civil works, plant, transport, accommodation, labour and maintenance.

Source: WA State Infrastructure Programme 2025 →

$10.3bn regional WA pipeline

$10.3 billion of that is earmarked for regional WA over four years — enough secondary activity for local civil contractors, logistics providers, workshops, accommodation and equipment-hire businesses to participate in delivery, not just the principal contractor.

Source: WA State Infrastructure Programme 2025 →

$1bn Strategic Industries Fund

The Strategic Industries Fund has been increased to $1 billion, backing strategic industrial areas and infrastructure for emerging industries — green energy, manufacturing and downstream processing among them.

Source: WA Government budget statement →

Kimberley & North West megaprojects

$22bn Australian Renewable Energy Hub

Planned across roughly 6,500km² in the East Pilbara. The Kimberley Development Commission puts it at an estimated $22 billion, with around 20,000 jobs expected across a 10-year construction period — manufacturing, equipment supply, construction, logistics and infrastructure. It sits in the Pilbara rather than the Kimberley, but its scale makes it relevant to North West supply chains servicing it from a Kimberley base or in partnership with Pilbara contractors.

Source: Kimberley Development Commission →

$64m Great Northern Highway & Pinga Street

A new four-way interchange, bridges and access infrastructure tied to Lumsden Point and over-size over-mass vehicle movements, completing in 2026. Projects that improve heavy-haul and OSOM connectivity are a useful early signal of coming demand for transport, heavy equipment support, maintenance and accommodation.

Source: Building for Tomorrow →

Broome capital works, 2026/27

$41.2 million capital works program. The Shire of Broome's 2026/27 Budget includes $41.2 million in capital works, more than 60% externally grant-funded — including the $23.5 million Walmanyjun Cable Beach Foreshore Redevelopment (Stage 2), the $2.2 million Old Broome Road upgrade, and ongoing investment in roads, recreation and community infrastructure.

Source: Shire of Broome — 2026/27 Annual Budget →  ·  Adopted budgets & financial reports →

Local capital works matter because they create room for businesses without the scale or project history for a major package — civil and earthworks subcontracting, traffic management, plant hire, haulage, electrical and mechanical services, labour hire, site services, materials supply, waste management, accommodation, and Aboriginal and local business participation. The question isn't just who won the project — it's what work needs to happen around it, and who's positioned to deliver it. See how this has played out on past Kimberley projects →

What it means for you

Four levels of opportunity — and why timing matters.

Treat the pipeline as a positioning opportunity, not a list of tenders to wait for. By the time a major project reaches open tender, much of the procurement decision has already happened informally — through market engagement, contractor selection and supply-chain planning.

1. Principal contractors

Identify who's likely to win the major construction, civil, energy, mining and infrastructure packages, and build relationships before tenders are released wherever you can.

2. Subcontracting

Work out which packages are likely to be subcontracted locally or regionally, and have a concise capability statement ready — location, capacity, equipment, workforce, experience, mobilisation.

3. Enabling services

Look past the construction scope. Major projects need accommodation, transport, freight, equipment, labour, maintenance, fabrication, waste services, security, catering, facilities, and environmental and professional services.

4. Local content & regional delivery

A credible local presence is commercially valuable where project owners and principal contractors are seeking regional participation — local workforce, Indigenous engagement, mobilisation capacity, safety systems, insurances and relevant experience. More on local content & participation →

The opportunity window: Identify → Map → Position → Engage → Prequalify → Tender → Deliver. Waiting for the tender advertisement often means entering the market after the most valuable relationship-building has already happened.

Getting procurement-ready

Before approaching a principal or tier-one contractor, have the fundamentals in place: ABN/ACN, insurances, WHS systems, relevant licences, workforce capacity, an equipment register, a capability statement, project references, financial information where required, prequalification registrations, and Indigenous/local participation information.
