Unbundling the scope

A head contract arrives packaged for the head contractor's convenience — large parcels, long durations, back-to-back risk and a single point of responsibility. Handed to the regional market in that form, most of it is untakeable. The businesses that could do the work cannot carry the value, the bonding, the insurance limits or the retention, so they decline, or they accept and then fail.

Unbundling is the commercial work of breaking that scope into packages the local market can actually take, without the head contractor giving up control of the programme. It is a pricing and risk-allocation exercise, not a goodwill one.

  • Package size. Set against what the business can fund between claims, not against what is tidy on a procurement schedule.
  • Package boundaries. Drawn along real trade lines, so a package does not quietly require a capability the region does not hold.
  • Risk allocation. Back-to-back terms tested against what a subcontractor of that size can actually bear — liquidated damages, design liability, latent conditions and insurance limits are where local businesses get caught.
  • Payment terms. Claim cycles and retention set so that performing the work does not require the business to bank the head contractor.
  • Sequencing. Packages timed around the wet season and around each other, so the programme does not depend on capacity that is committed elsewhere.
  • What stays bundled. Some scope should not be broken up. Saying so early is more useful than discovering it at award.

The test. A package is correctly drawn when a capable local business can price it, fund it, insure it and finish it without the head contractor carrying the difference. Anything else is a participation number that will not survive delivery.

Who can actually deliver it

Unbundling is only worth doing against a supplier base someone has checked. Most supplier information available to a contractor pricing Kimberley work is self-reported and unchecked, and the gap between the two is where participation commitments go wrong.

A directory tells you

  • The business exists
  • What it says it does
  • An ABN and a phone number
  • Whatever it entered on a registration form

The register records

  • Capability confirmed against actual work performed
  • Tickets, licences and competencies sighted
  • Insurances current and sighted
  • Plant held, and its condition and availability
  • Present capacity — what they can take on now
  • Rate history from Nausicaa's own market work

The Kimberley does not have the supplier depth of the Pilbara, and the difference is not evenly spread. Setting package boundaries and participation targets that survive close-out scrutiny means knowing which categories the region genuinely holds and which it does not.

Genuine depth

  • Labour hire and site services
  • Civil plant operations and earthworks
  • Logistics and transport
  • Camp catering and facilities
  • Security and site access
  • Land management and environmental works

Thin or absent

  • Complex structural steel fabrication
  • Specialist electrical and instrumentation
  • Most licensed trades at scale
  • High-volume specialist plant

Where the question is what a package should cost rather than who can take it, the rate build and market sounding work is described under tendering and estimating.

Pre-qualification as a commercial function

Frequently treated as administrative — collecting SWMS, insurance certificates and licences. Administered properly it establishes which subcontractors hold the WHS management systems your head contract requires, and which carry the financial capacity for the subcontract value you need to place with them. In a market this size the pool ranges from well-capitalised operators to sole traders carrying more risk than they can manage, and a process that ignores financial capacity builds the wrong panel — then discovers it mid-programme.

WAIPS, APP and IPP

These are contractual obligations with reporting requirements attached, not policy aspirations, and they are assessed at close-out by people who were not in the room when the commitment was made.

  • WAIPS — the WA Industry Participation Strategy. State Government contracts above threshold carry mandatory local participation commitments, submitted as an Industry Participation Plan and reported through delivery.
  • APP — the WA Aboriginal Procurement Policy. Applies to State Government contracts and sets Aboriginal business participation requirements alongside the Social Procurement Framework.
  • IPP — the Commonwealth Indigenous Procurement Policy. Applies where the funding or the contract is Commonwealth, and carries its own supplier and employment requirements, reported on its own terms. Grant-funded Kimberley works frequently sit under this one rather than the State frameworks, and the two are not interchangeable.

Which framework applies is a function of the funding source and the contract value, and it is worth establishing before a plan is written rather than after. Thresholds and requirements change; they are confirmed against the current published policy for each engagement rather than assumed.

Why remote-written plans fail

  • Suppliers nominated who do not have the capacity to perform at the level promised
  • Targets set against a supplier base nobody has verified
  • Trade categories chosen from the list above where the region holds no capability at all
  • The gap between what was promised and what is available then gets managed for the life of the project
  • Plans returned for revision cost programme time that a tender submission date does not have

Failure to meet commitments at close-out carries commercial and reputational consequences, and it follows the contractor into the next evaluation.

What a credible participation plan contains

  • Description of the works, package by package — which is why the unbundling comes first
  • Identification of WA industry capability in each trade package
  • A methodology for engaging suppliers, not a statement of intent
  • Measurable commitments with defined reporting milestones
  • A mechanism for addressing gaps where targets are not met

Plans that do not address each element are returned for revision.

The part most plans miss

Weekly reporting. A participation plan that is only reported at close-out is a plan nobody can correct. Weekly tracking is what turns a commitment into a result — and what produces the evidence trail when performance is questioned.

Aboriginal participation

  • Compliance requires more than directing work to an Aboriginal-owned business
  • The obligation extends to structuring the engagement so the business can actually perform — which is the unbundling work above, applied to a specific package
  • That means confirming capacity, setting appropriate subcontract terms, and supporting capability gaps that would otherwise prevent delivery
  • It requires a conversation that cannot be had from a Perth office

On-ground engagement with Traditional Owner representatives brings heritage, access and cultural considerations forward to where they can still be designed around, rather than leaving them to surface as programme risk. Ranger groups hold land management, environmental and local operating knowledge that is directly relevant to the works and rarely reflected in a supplier register.
